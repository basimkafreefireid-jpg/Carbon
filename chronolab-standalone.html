03 / Understand the science

From sample to story.

A good result is not just a number. It is a measurement interpreted in context.

STEP 01

Check the material

Radiocarbon dating measures carbon-14 in once-living material. Stone, most metals, and the age of a building are not directly dated this way.

STEP 02

Measure carbon-14

A specialist laboratory prepares the sample and measures its radiocarbon content, reporting an age or activity alongside uncertainty.

STEP 03

Calibrate the result

Atmospheric carbon-14 has changed over time. A calibration curve translates conventional radiocarbon age into calendar-age probability ranges.

06 / The essentials

What is carbon dating?

Radiocarbon dating estimates when suitable once-living material stopped exchanging carbon with its environment. It is a scientific measurement, not a visual guess.

Carbon dating, also called radiocarbon dating or carbon-14 dating, measures the remaining radioactive carbon-14 in suitable organic samples. Living organisms exchange carbon with their surroundings. After that exchange ends, carbon-14 decays, so a laboratory can measure the remaining amount and estimate a conventional radiocarbon age.

Carbon-14 has a physical half-life of about 5,730 years. However, a raw radiocarbon age is not automatically a calendar date: atmospheric carbon-14 has varied over time, and the sample's environment can affect its result. Specialists use an appropriate calibration curve and sample context to estimate calendar-age ranges.

Radiocarbon dating is commonly used with suitable wood, charcoal, bone collagen, seeds, textiles, and other organic material. It does not directly date ordinary stone or most metal. For objects made from mixed materials, the sample itself and its archaeological context matter.

04 / Read results responsibly

Accuracy, calibration & limitations.

A radiocarbon result is a scientific measurement that needs sample context and calibration before it can be interpreted as a calendar date.

Conventional radiocarbon age is not the same as calendar age. Laboratories report a radiocarbon age using standardized conventions. Because atmospheric carbon-14 has changed over time, specialists compare the measurement with an appropriate calibration curve to estimate calendar-age probability ranges.

Uncertainty matters. A laboratory uncertainty describes part of the measurement uncertainty; it does not automatically include contamination, sample selection, reservoir effects, or all archaeological uncertainties. A simple interval displayed by this calculator is not a calibrated probability range.

Sample choice matters. A sample must be suitable, adequately preserved, and prepared appropriately. Contamination or dating material that is not directly related to the event of interest can lead to misleading interpretations.

Age limits matter. As samples become very old, little carbon-14 remains and background contamination becomes increasingly important. Post-1950 samples may require specialist methods because atmospheric nuclear testing changed carbon-14 levels.

Use the right method. Ordinary rocks and most metals cannot be directly dated with radiocarbon dating. Other techniques may be more suitable depending on the material, the question, and the geological or archaeological context.

For a real sample, consult the laboratory that performed the measurement or a qualified specialist. This website is an educational aid and does not replace laboratory analysis, professional interpretation, or a validated calibration program.

05 / Quick answers

Good questions to ask.

Age estimation has limits. These basics help keep the result in perspective.

Can I find an object's exact age from a photo?

No. A photo can help describe an object or identify possible dating methods, but it cannot measure carbon-14. A laboratory must analyze suitable material. Read our guide to materials that may be dated.

Does radiocarbon dating work on anything?

No. It is designed for suitable carbon-containing material that was once part of a living organism. Stone and metal objects usually need other evidence or dating methods. See the sample suitability guide.

What does “years BP” mean?

BP stands for “before present,” where “present” is fixed at AD 1950 in radiocarbon science. A conventional radiocarbon age is not automatically the same as a calendar year.

Why can one result have multiple date ranges?

Calibration curves can intersect a measurement in several places. The result may therefore include multiple calendar-age ranges with different probabilities, rather than one exact date.

What can make a result misleading?

Contamination, mixed samples, old wood, reservoir effects, and uncertain archaeological context can all affect interpretation. A qualified laboratory or specialist should assess the sample and its context.
